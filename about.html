My story

How I got into building things

My first real project started with a question from a contest prompt: could a cheap wearable help people with Parkinson's disease track their symptoms? In 2021 I built Park It! on the AWS IoT EduKit, streaming skin-response, audio, and motion data to the cloud. It became a finalist in the AWS Reinventing Healthy Spaces contest. Most of my projects since then have started the same way, with a specific problem someone has, and ended with hardware and software I built myself.

In high school at Bergen County Academies' Academy of Engineering and Design Technology in New Jersey, I kept that up. I built a bike-mounted pedestrian detector on a Xilinx Kria FPGA (BikeWatch), built LoRa sensors to catch illegal sand mining (MonSand), trained a vision model to count walrus for researchers (WalrusEye), and turned a community Little Free Library into a solar-powered, touchscreen-equipped build. The bugs that took me longest to fix were almost always at the boundary between hardware and software, and I found I liked chasing them.

I also got into research. My project MACAWED used reinforcement learning to defend distributed machine-learning systems from malicious clients and won first place in Math and CS at the New Jersey Academy of Science. At Princeton's Puchalla Biophysics Lab, I replaced a 2D electromagnetic simulation with a 3D pipeline. I scaled it to 5,000 cores on a Linux HPC cluster, and its agreement with experimental data improved by 55%.

At Georgia Tech I'm working lower in the hardware stack. With Silicon Jackets I designed a RISC-V-style processor in SystemVerilog and took it through a full RTL-to-GDSII flow. With Georgia Tech Solar Racing I work on power electronics for a 200 V battery-test load. I still build software too: my Java app Duke's Garden won the Grand Prize in Oracle's Modern Java in the Wild contest this fall.

Beyond the bench

Competitive programming has been my other constant. I captained the Bergen County Academies team for three years and grew it fivefold by writing 24 original contests and running more than 30 practice sessions. Watching teammates work through problems I'd written was as fun as winning anything myself. I also speak English, Punjabi, and Hindi fluently.
